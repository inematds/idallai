How to use this central

By the end of this lesson, you’ll find, in less than a minute, which workflow in this hub solves the problem you have right now—and know how to decide whether it’s worth trying today or saving for later.

You’ve completed the earlier courses in this program and know how to do the essentials: generate an image with the right light, keep a character consistent, put together a clip. But real work brings requests that go beyond the basics—a complete character sheet, an ad made from a single photo, a high-impact scene. That’s what this hub is for: tested workflows, ready to adapt, so you don’t have to reinvent the technique on your own.

01 This hub is a filing cabinet, not a self-contained lesson

Every previous course in this program follows a sequence: you start with lesson 1 and finish with the final lesson, in an order where each builds on the last. This hub works differently—each lesson here is an independent entry point, a ready-to-use workflow you can open directly without needing the others.

It's the difference between reading a novel and consulting a recipe card file: a novel calls for order, from chapter 1 to the end; with the recipe cards, you open the one you need today, and the file keeps growing over time, never quite "finished".

The photographer setting up her own studio already lives by this logic: she keeps each lighting setup that worked well during a session in a folder—not as a story with a beginning and an end, but as a collection she consults and adds to year after year.

The course ends. The notebook doesn’t—it only grows.

02 Each entry follows the same format

So you don’t waste time figuring out each new lesson, they all follow the same reading format: a sentence telling you exactly what you’ll know how to do, why it matters now, the step-by-step workflow, and, when available, an original video in English—always optional, because the Portuguese text is already complete on its own.

And since this hub is made almost entirely of tool, not foundation — the distinction you’ve used since course 1 — each workflow includes the date it was checked. Tool screens change; the technique behind them is usually still valid even after the screen changes.

The owner of a bike shop about to launch its summer collection scans the hub this way: he reads only the promise sentence for each lesson, without opening any yet, looking for the one that addresses his current problem—promoting new parts quickly without hiring anyone to do it.

03 How to decide what to try first

With several workflows available, it’s tempting to try the flashiest one—the one that looks most impressive at first glance. That’s the wrong criterion. The right one is simpler: which workflow solves a problem you already have this week, not which one looks coolest.

After choosing, test it on one of your own low-risk projects before taking it to a client—this way, you learn the workflow’s limits without putting a real deliverable at risk.

Before

The social media manager who handles a Pilates studio’s account tries every new workflow that comes along, in the order she sees them—with no criteria and no extra time at the end of the week.

After

She reads the promises of every lesson, chooses the one that solves the real problem this week (consistent before-and-after posts for the launch of a new class), and tests only that one.

The payoff: the same free time, invested in the workflow that will actually be used—not the flashiest one.

Course 7 · Lesson 2

The sheet for the character

By the end of this lesson, you’ll turn a single reference photo into a complete set of angles and close-ups of the same character — ready to use as the basis for any campaign or video.

You already know how to generate six angles in a single grid (course 1). But a real project—a catalog, a series of posts, a short film—needs more than a grid: it needs a complete set, with studio close-ups, ready to deliver. Doing that manually, one image at a time, takes all afternoon.

↓ role to study

01 A reference photo is not a character sheet

In Course 1, you learned to generate a grid with six angles in a single image—great for a standalone scene. But a project that runs for weeks—a whole catalog, a series of posts, a mini-film—needs more than that: it needs a character profile complete, in the same spirit as an actor’s portfolio: multiple views, multiple close-ups, all together, ready to consult whenever you need.

The photographer who works with a barbershop sees this difference in practice: one reference photo of the barber is enough for a post; a whole before-and-after catalog, with the same face in several angles and framings, needs a reference sheet—not one more standalone image.

02 The workflow: one photo goes in, a complete profile comes out

This may sound complicated, but it works like a fixed recipe: you provide the main ingredient—the character photo—and the workflow already knows the other steps, without you having to decide each one. Tools like Magnific exist for exactly this: you connect the character image, run a single action, and get back a ready-made set with multiple views, close-ups, and references, all with the same identity.

The plant shop owner uses this workflow like this: uploads a single photo of the sales associate who appears in the shop's posts and gets a variety of close-ups and angles of her — enough material for a month's worth of content, without always repeating the same image.

The process, in four steps

  1. Choose 1 clear, well-lit photo of the character—face visible, simple background.
  2. Send this photo to the character profile workflow you have available.
  3. Run the action—without writing anything besides the photo.
  4. Download the complete sheet: views, close-ups, and references, ready to use.

03 When to use the reference sheet and when the grid is enough

The grid from course 1 still works for everyday use: a single scene, today’s post, six angles in one generation. The full sheet comes in when the character is going to go back — across multiple projects over weeks or months, when you don’t want to redo the consistency work every time.

The social media manager who handles the barbershop account decides this way: for this Friday’s post, the course’s quick grid is enough; for the quarterly services catalog launch, she runs the full brief once and reuses the same set for months.

04 Check before you submit

The photographer who received the barbershop’s complete reference sheet doesn’t send it straight to the client: she reviews it frame by frame first, comparing faces, clothes, and lighting across them.

Common mistake

Use the entire sheet without checking it frame by frame. Every now and then, a close-up slips through with a slightly different face from the others—the full sheet isn’t perfect by default. Check it before using it in production and discard only the frame that’s off; there’s no need to redo the whole sheet.

Course 7 · Lesson 3

The plan that leads into the next

By the end of this lesson, you’ll link short AI-generated video clips into a sequence with no visible cuts—using the last frame of each clip as the reference for the next.

You already know how to generate a single clip (course 1). The problem comes up when you need more than a few seconds of footage: generating separate clips and joining them later often breaks the magic—the clothes change color, the skin loses texture, the movement stutters at the cut. There’s a simple technique that solves this: chain, don’t patch.

watch this lesson on video (English · optional)

↓ role to study

01 Each new clip starts from scratch too

The same cause from lesson 1 of course 1 shows up again here, now in video: a generator doesn’t remember the previous clip. If you ask it to create the second clip from scratch, even with a detailed description of the character, the lighting shifts, the clothes lose some texture, the framing drifts—small differences that, together, give away the cut.

The plant shop owner noticed this when generating three separate clips of the same sales associate explaining a different care tip in each video: by the third scene, her apron had already changed to a different shade of green — no one asked for it, but it happened.

02 The technique: the last frame becomes the reference for the next

The professional solution isn’t to describe everything again in words—it’s to provide the image itself. Extract the last frame from the clip you just generated and use that image as the starting reference for the next prompt. Since the starting point is literally the same image, the lighting, clothing, and skin texture stay consistent from one clip to the next.

Common mistake

Trying to maintain continuity by only describing the previous clip in text. A word-based description always leaves room for the AI to interpret things differently—the only way to guarantee the exact same lighting is to provide the image of the last frame, not a summary of it.

The photographer who works with the barbershop uses this technique to record a short before-and-after video series: the last frame of the “before” haircut becomes the reference for the next clip, and the transition between the two is invisible.

03 Skin texture and natural movement still matter

What you learned in lesson 2 of course 1—that too much perfection looks fake—applies here just as much in motion: skin without a single pore, an expression that never changes, a gesture that jumps from one pose to another without passing through the motions in between. All of this gives away raw AI, even in a well-connected sequence.

Asking for natural microexpressions and smooth movement — instead of letting the generator decide on its own — is what separates a scene that feels acted from one that looks like a puppet. The social media manager who edits the barbershop’s footage knows how to spot this in the first cut: when the movement “jumps,” she asks for another take with a more specific pacing instruction.

04 Connecting three or more shots

The process, linking the shots

  1. Generate the first clip as usual, with the complete character, lighting, and setting description.
  2. Save (or extract) the last frame of that clip as an image.
  3. Use this image as the starting reference for your next video prompt, keeping the same clothing and setting description.
  4. Repeat the process for each following clip; assemble the final sequence in the order they were generated.

The plant shop owner wraps up the series like this: three clips with different care tips, each starting exactly where the previous one ended — the same sales associate, the same apron, the same late-afternoon light from start to finish.

Practice now 0/4 done

Connect two clips in a sequence with no cut

Leave with two clips linked into one continuous sequence—in ~14 minutes.

Each generated clip is a new file — chaining them doesn’t delete or overwrite anything. If the transition is visible, just adjust the reference-frame description and generate again.

You linked two clips into a continuous sequence—the technique that separates raw AI video from AI video that feels cinematic.

Highlight any passage to turn it into a review card.

Summary

  • Each new video clip also starts with no memory of the previous one — chaining them solves that; patching things in the edit doesn’t.
  • The technique: the last frame of one clip becomes the starting reference for the next, keeping the lighting, clothing, and skin texture consistent.
  • Skin texture and micro-expressions still matter here just as much as they did in the first image: real imperfections are more convincing than plastic perfection.
  • The result is a sequence that feels like one continuous shot, even though it’s made of several clips generated separately.

From photo to ad, in one flow only

By the end of this lesson, you’ll turn a single product photo into a complete vertical ad — planned, storyboarded, and filmed through an automated workflow — ready to publish.

Making a product ad from scratch—choosing the shots, writing the script, generating each scene, putting it all together—takes a whole day that a small business doesn’t have to spare. There’s a workflow that does all of that on its own, using one photo and two lines of text from you.

↓ role to study

01 Agency work, distilled into a workflow

A real product ad comes from a multi-step process: someone analyzes the product as an advertising professional would, plans each scene, designs a storyboard and only then films. Today, there’s a workflow that handles this entire chain on its own, starting from a photo and a short brief from you.

The owner of a bakery launching a new cake can picture this without hiring anyone: take a photo of the cake, write two lines about the mood you want, and the workflow returns a ready-to-use ad — without them having to decide on a single camera shot.

02 What you deliver: one photo and two lines

This is as simple as filling out a label: you write the product name and a sentence about the mood you want, provide the photo, and the workflow decides the rest on its own, scene by scene.

Product: <o nome do seu produto>
Mood: <o clima/estilo que você quer — ex: "luxury, warm evening light">

The process, in four steps

  1. Photograph the product on its own, against a simple background with good lighting.
  2. Write the product name and the desired mood, in no more than two lines.
  3. Send the photo and text to the automatic ad workflow.
  4. Wait: it returns the finished vertical ad, scene by scene.

The social media manager for a Pilates studio uses this workflow to announce a new mat: a product photo, two lines setting the mood, and the rest of the agency work comes ready-made.

03 Why the same product appears in every frame

This workflow carries the same principle from lesson 3 under the hood: each new scene starts connected to the previous one instead of starting from scratch — so your product’s packaging, label, and color stay identical from the first to the last second of the ad, without you having to ask for it.

For the bakery owner, this consistency is what makes the ad look professional: the same cake, with the same frosting, recognizable in every shot—not a sequence of unrelated images that happen to show a similar cake.

04 The result is only as good as the input photo

Common mistake

Sending a bad photo thinking the workflow will "fix" everything. The workflow inherits the flaws of the original photo—crooked angle, cluttered background, harsh light—and they show up the same way in the final ad. Review the product photo itself (lighting and background), as you learned in lesson 1 of course 1, before submitting it.

Before any automated ad, the photographer reviewing the bakery’s material always checks the same thing: could the opening photo stand alone as the final image? If so, it’s ready to feed into the workflow.

None of your photos are altered—the workflow always creates a new video. If the result doesn’t convince you, change only the mood phrase and run it again, with no extra manual work.

You have a complete vertical ad, ready to publish, made from one photo and two lines of text.

  • An automated workflow can take on the work of an entire agency: analyze the product, plan the shots, design the storyboard, and direct the generation.
  • Your input is minimal—a photo and two lines—because the workflow decides everything else on its own.
  • Product consistency across frames is what makes an ad look professional, not homemade.
  • The result is only as good as the original photo—poor lighting and background in the input still look poor in the output.

Course 7 · Lesson 5

The recipe for the scene that holds the gaze

By the end of this lesson, you’ll write a high-impact video prompt using the same four-part structure found in the most widely shared scenes—and adapt that structure to any scene of your own.

You already know how to describe a shot (courses 1 and 2). There’s a level beyond that: longer, more ambitious video prompts—a striking store opening, a dish coming out of the kitchen in style—that follow their own formula, timed second by second. Without that formula, an ambitious prompt becomes a list of disconnected ideas that the AI can’t string together into a single scene.

watch this lesson on video (English · optional)

↓ role to study

01 Every impactful scene has the same anatomy

Behind every video scene that really grabs you—the kind that makes someone stop scrolling—is a fixed structure, repeated by people who write this kind of prompt: a scene clock (what happens second by second), the camera language (what kind of shot, what movement), a block of visual style (light, lens, color) and a block of sound (the scene’s soundscape). Four parts, always in this order of thought.

The social media manager at a bookstore can feel the difference: asking for "a pretty video of the store opening" returns something generic; breaking the same idea into four parts returns a scene with pacing, planned like a real commercial.

02 Parts 1 and 2: the scene’s timing and camera language

The first part divides the scene into time blocks—three seconds here, another three there—and describes what happens in each stretch. The second names the shot type and camera movement for each block, the same way you’ve already learned to describe a single shot in course 1.

[0–3s] Wide shot, slow dolly-in: a vitrine da livraria acende as luzes ao anoitecer.
[3–6s] Close-up: o letreiro "aberto" balança na porta, sino tocando.
[6–9s] Medium shot: o dono folheia as primeiras páginas de um livro novo na mesa de destaque.

Common mistake

Write the entire scene as one continuous paragraph, without dividing it into time blocks. Without this division, the AI tries to fit everything into a single instant and the scene loses its rhythm—the opening, middle, and climax all become one jumbled thing.

The bookstore owner tested both formats before deciding: the continuous paragraph returned a static scene; the same events divided into three time blocks returned a scene with a visible beginning, middle, and end.

03 Parts 3 and 4: visual style and sound design

The last two parts complete the prompt: a short block of style (the light, lens, color palette you want) and a block of sound (the soundscape — music, an effect, or intentional silence). They always come last, as a finishing touch on the scene already described.

Style: cinematic warm light, shallow depth of field, film grain, golden hour tones
Sound: sino da porta, passos leves, música instrumental suave crescendo no final

The sound block is what's most often missing from beginner requests — and it's exactly what turns a silent, dull scene into one that feels like it has its own soundtrack. The photographer helping the bookstore plan its launch video always reviews this block last, before sending the request.

04 Build your own four-part prompt

The process, putting the request together

  1. Write a one-sentence summary of your scene—what happens, from beginning to end.
  2. Divide it into time blocks (for example, 0–3s, 3–6s, 6–9s), describing what happens in each one.
  3. For each block, name the shot type and camera movement.
  4. Close with a visual style block and a sound block.

The bookstore owner puts their request together like this: writes the launch scene in one sentence, divides it into three time blocks, names the shots, and wraps up with the visual and sound mood that fits the store's audience.

Summary

  • Every impactful scene uses the same four-part anatomy: timed beats, camera language, visual style, and sound design.
  • Dividing into time blocks is what gives the scene rhythm—a continuous paragraph makes the AI lose the beat.
  • The sound block is often the most overlooked, and it's what separates a silent scene from one that really holds your attention.
  • The same formula works for any scene, from your product to your business—only the content changes; the structure stays the same.
